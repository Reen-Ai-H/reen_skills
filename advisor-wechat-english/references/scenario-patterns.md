# Scenario Patterns

These patterns were distilled from real advisor-WeChat exchanges. Match the intent and relationship, not the wording mechanically. Keep the user's actual facts and current conversational context.

## Ask for an Opinion

For a general request:

> When you have a chance, I'd really appreciate your thoughts on my choices.

> Do you have any suggestions or recommendations?

After sending a document or screenshot:

> When you have a moment, could you take another look and let me know what you think?

`Could you please take another look?` is polite and acceptable in English. It is slightly more formal and request-like. In an ongoing WeChat conversation, `When you have a moment, could you take another look?` is often warmer and puts less pressure on the advisor. Do not translate “麻烦教授” literally or add `Professor` to every message.

## Send a Course List or Other Attachment

> After considering your suggestions, my own interests, and the detailed course information, this is the course list I've come up with.

> When you have a chance, could you take another look at it?

If the user is still asking for advice, avoid calling it the `final selection`. That can imply the decision is already closed.

For an analysis document or company shortlist:

> I've sent you my analysis and shortlist. When you have time, I'd really appreciate your thoughts on my choices and any suggestions you may have.

## Clarify a Misunderstanding

If a curriculum spreadsheet was mistaken for a final selection:

> The Excel file is my program's curriculum plan, not my final course selection.

> I only need to choose one or two courses from this section to meet the credit requirement.

To explain courses inside and outside the plan:

> I need to choose some courses from my curriculum plan, but I can also take additional courses outside it as electives.

Keep corrections direct and neutral. Do not imply that the advisor failed to understand.

## Correct a Mistake

> I just realized I made a mistake. I can't select this course either.

> It seems there aren't any relevant courses available to me this semester.

> I'll see if I can take a relevant course next semester. In the meantime, I can study the relevant topics online as needed.

One clear apology or acknowledgement is enough. Do not turn a small correction into a formal apology.

## Preserve Uncertainty

> As far as I know, the rest of the program is mostly the same as the regular Electronic Information master's program.

> However, the program was only introduced two years ago, so I may not know all the details yet.

Choose the confidence marker that matches the evidence:

- `The syllabus says ...` for explicit written information.
- `It seems ...` for an interpretation.
- `As far as I know ...` for limited personal knowledge.
- `I'm not sure whether ...` for genuine uncertainty.

Do not make a tentative thought sound like a settled fact.

## Explain a Special Program

> The main difference is that, in the second year, we need to complete a placement at a company and write a report on the experience.

> As far as I know, most of the other requirements are the same as those in the regular Electronic Information master's program.

> The program only started two years ago, though, so I may not know every detail yet.

## Summarize Technical Course Content

Lead with the distinction the advisor cares about:

> I checked the syllabus. Distributed learning is only one two-hour topic out of the 32-hour course.

> The course mainly focuses on deep-learning hardware architectures, software stacks, system optimization, and model deployment on platforms such as Raspberry Pi and ARM.

Ask for advice without overstating the match:

> It seems related to what you mentioned, although it may not focus specifically on microcontrollers. Would you recommend taking it?

For a course involving a Raspberry Pi:

> When I saw that one of the projects uses a Raspberry Pi, I added it to my list right away.

## Respond to a Useful Reminder

> Yeah, that's a good point. I'll double-check the course content.

This is more natural in chat than a formal statement such as `Thank you for reminding me of this important issue.`

## Discuss a Challenging Course

When uncertain:

> I'm still not sure whether it would be the right choice for me, since it seems to be a very demanding course.

When enthusiastic:

> I know it'll be challenging, but I'm happy to take it on because it looks really interesting.

Translate “硬核” according to meaning as `demanding`, `intensive`, or `challenging`. Use `hardcore` only if the user deliberately wants playful slang.

## Explain Interest in Product-Facing Work

> One reason I'm interested in these companies is that their work seems more application- and product-oriented, rather than purely research-focused.

> It also involves understanding user needs and bringing technology closer to real-world applications.

> Since I have some previous experience in user growth, I'm particularly interested in this kind of work.

Do not use `front desk` or `front-end` for “偏前面、贴近用户” unless the work is literally reception or front-end software development. Prefer `user-facing`, `product-oriented`, `application-oriented`, or `closer to real users`.

## Arrange or Reschedule a Meeting

> I should be free before 2 p.m. or after 4:30 p.m., whichever works best for you.

If useful, briefly explain the conflict:

> There's a university meeting on talent policies tomorrow that I'd really like to attend.

For a postponement:

> No problem. Tomorrow afternoon works for me.

State the available windows clearly. One light softener such as `whichever works best for you` adds respect without making the message ceremonial.

## Say There Is No Rush

> No rush at all. Please take your time.

If mentioning a side project:

> No rush at all. BTW, I'm also working on a small LLM-based novel-writing project, so I'll have something to work on in the meantime.

Avoid `I've got plenty to do` unless the user genuinely wants to signal being busy. It can discourage the advisor from contacting them.

## Discuss a Lab Desk or Workstation

> I haven't been assigned a workstation yet.

> As far as I know, none of my friends or classmates have been assigned one either. I think the workstations will be allocated later.

After visiting the room:

> Yesterday, I went to see the room with one of my senior labmates. It seems there may not be a desk available after all. The room is quite cramped.

Use `desk` or `workstation` for 工位. `Office` suggests a separate room. Use `senior labmate` or `senior student` for 师兄 when the relationship matters.

## Respond to a Recommendation or Contact

> Okay, I'll ask Meng Yi about the tutorials. Thanks for letting me know!

This acknowledges both the action and the helpful information without over-thanking.

## Thank the Advisor

Useful compact forms:

> Got it, thanks for letting me know!

> Thanks for checking!

> Thanks for your advice!

> Thanks a lot!

> Sounds good, thanks!

`thx` can occasionally fit a relaxed exchange with a young advisor, but `Thanks` is the safer default and is barely longer. Avoid uppercase `THX` and uncommon `tx`.

## Show Enthusiasm

> I'm really looking forward to our first meeting tomorrow!

One exclamation mark is enough. Do not inflate ordinary interest into exaggerated excitement.

## Interpret Non-Native Advisor English

The advisor may also write English as a non-native speaker. Infer the likely practical meaning from context instead of criticizing grammar.

Example:

> Tomorrow's meeting will be online, but I give it to you for your information.

Likely meaning: tomorrow's meeting is online, and the advisor is sharing the office location for future reference.

A sufficient reply is:

> Got it, thanks for letting me know!

When interpretation is uncertain, separate the literal meaning from the likely intended meaning and say that the latter is an inference.
